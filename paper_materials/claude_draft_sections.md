## 3. Method

### 3.1 Research design

The system is designed as a formative feedback tool, intended to support learners when they write outside of class, without access to teacher feedback. This differs from studies of automated essay scoring, where the aim is holistic or analytic evaluation of learner performance. The key criterion for our system is therefore not the agreement of AI-assigned scores with human scores, but its usefulness to students in improving their own writing. Usefulness, in turn, depends on two factors: which aspects of writing the feedback addresses, and how accurate the feedback is when judged against expert feedback.

The first factor is the choice of feedback categories. The system gives feedback on five linguistic elements: grammar corrections, vocabulary suggestions, coherence, expressiveness (including elaboration), and content completeness. [Linguistic rationale for these categories -- to be written by the supervising teacher; see Section 3.3.]

The second factor is the accuracy of the feedback. A feedback item is accurate if it flags a real error, provides a correct revision, and explains the rule involved. To develop and evaluate accuracy, this study adopted a design-based research (DBR) approach, in which an educational intervention is developed through iterative cycles of design, enactment, and analysis in an authentic setting, in close collaboration with a linguistics expert (Design-Based Research Collective, 2003; McKenney & Reeves, 2019 [verify]). Accuracy was addressed in two ways. During development, the course teacher reviewed the system's feedback on real learner texts item by item, marking each item as correct or identifying what was wrong with it. Each critique was addressed by a specific change to the system, and the revised system was reviewed again. Across this process, the critiques fell into five main categories of refinement: (1) accuracy of flagged items, including corrections of text that was already correct and correct revisions with missing or wrong explanations; (2) errors the system failed to flag; (3) reference in coherence, such as unclear pronouns or characters not properly introduced; (4) missing content in retellings of stories with a set storyline; and (5) separating vocabulary errors, which must be corrected, from vocabulary suggestions, which are optional.

> To test whether these refinements improved the accuracy of the feedback, the final system was evaluated in a controlled comparison (RQ3). Each text received feedback under three conditions, all using the same language model and settings: (1) the baseline instructions, written before any expert review; (2) the final instructions, which incorporate the five refinement categories, without retrieval of the teacher's reference material; and (3) the full system, with both the final instructions and retrieval. >
> Each refinement category was measured against the teacher's own work on the test texts: (1) the proportion of flagged items that were correct, with a correct revision and explanation; (2) the proportion of the teacher's corrections that the system also flagged; (3) the same proportion for the teacher's corrections involving reference; (4) agreement between the scenes the system reported as missing and the scenes the teacher marked as missing; and (5) the proportion of errors from the teacher's vocabulary error list that the system marked as errors rather than as suggestions. The teacher coded each feedback item without knowing which condition had produced it, and a second Chinese language instructor coded a subset of items to check the reliability of the coding. Each condition was run three times on each text to check the consistency of the output. Sections 3.6 and 3.7 give the full procedure.

This study evaluates the quality of the feedback, not its effect on learners' writing over time. Measuring that effect, for example through changes in learners' analytic scores, is the aim of a follow-up study.

Two people carried out the study. The supervising teacher, who holds a PhD in linguistics and has taught Chinese as a second language for 20 years, acted as the linguistics expert: she defined the rubric, wrote the reference materials, reviewed the system's feedback during development, and coded the feedback in the evaluation. The first author designed and implemented the system.

No model parameters were changed at any point. "Refinement" in this study refers only to changes in what the model is given to read when it generates feedback: its instructions and the reference material retrieved for each learner text. We avoid the term "training", which readers may take to mean fine-tuning.

Both the development and the evaluation depend on expert data that existed before any AI feedback was generated: the teacher's rubric for the assignment, her corrections of learner texts, and model versions of the stories. Section 3.2 describes this data and the teaching context it comes from.

### 3.2 Context and expert data

**Teaching context.** The system was developed for a [level] Chinese as a second language course at [university], taught by the supervising teacher. A central writing assignment in the course is narrative retelling: students watch a short video of a traditional Chinese story and retell it in Chinese in their own words. Four stories are used: 神农尝百草 (Shennong tastes the hundred herbs), 孟母三迁 (Mencius's mother moves three times), 嫦娥奔月 (Chang'e flies to the moon), and 张骞出使西域 (Zhang Qian's mission to the Western Regions). Each story has a fixed storyline, which the teacher has divided into required scenes (between five and thirteen per story). Before the system existed, students wrote their retellings outside class and the teacher returned line-by-line corrections, together with notes on any required scenes that were missing. The system is intended to give students comparable feedback while they write, before the teacher sees their work.

In the system, students work scene by scene. For each scene they first fill in optional planning prompts (characters, objects, actions, descriptions), which are not sent to the AI, and then write the scene in Chinese. They receive feedback on that scene, may revise it once, and move on to the next. When the story is complete, they receive feedback on the content of the story as a whole. Students can also write a free story of their own in the same format. Section 3.4 describes the system in detail.

**Expert data.** Before any AI feedback was generated, three kinds of expert data were available, all produced by or for the teacher as part of her normal teaching (Table 1). We refer to these as the base data, to distinguish them from the teacher's critiques of AI output, which were produced later during development (Section 3.5).

The first is the teacher's rubric for the assignment, which defines the aspects of writing her feedback addresses. It is the source of the feedback categories described in Section 3.3.

The second is the teacher's list of required scenes for each story, together with a model version of each story. The two serve different purposes. The required-scene list states which scenes a complete retelling must contain, and is the reference for content completeness, which is judged once for the whole story. The model stories, written by [author -- verify] and 370 to 610 characters long, supply the details within each scene that the list does not: for example, that the man who helps 神农 is dressed in blue and gives him 灵芝, which is shaped like an umbrella. They are the reference for expressiveness, which is addressed scene by scene: the system draws on these details to suggest how a student could describe a scene more fully.

The third is a set of 103 learner retellings with the teacher's feedback: 51 retellings of 孟母三迁 and 52 of 神农尝百草, averaging about 220 and 250 characters respectively. [Verify: written texts or transcribed oral retellings.] The teacher's feedback on these texts takes two forms. The first is 825 line-level corrections, each giving the learner's original phrase, the corrected phrase, and usually a short explanation of the rule involved, written in the teacher's own shorthand (for example "了completed" or "看 look vs. 看到 see"). These corrections cover grammar, word choice, and coherence, and are the reference for those categories. The second is 97 notes on content: 89 identify required scenes that the retelling leaves out (for example "Miss 1 scene: 神农 found 茶 and took a record"), 5 identify wrong information, and 3 identify problems of coherence. These are the reference for content completeness. In the learner texts, students are identified only by number; no names or other personal information appear.

### 3.4 System architecture

The architecture of the system serves the two parts of usefulness set out in Section 3.1: it delivers feedback on the five linguistic elements, and it allows that feedback to be made more accurate through repeated expert review. Both purposes depend on six essential components, which connect in a cycle (Figure 2).

**Components and how they connect.** The cycle begins with the (1) expert documents: the teacher's corrected retellings, required-scene lists, and model stories from the base data, and the verdicts, vocabulary error list, and coherence rules from the critique data (Section 3.2). These expert documents are converted by a (2) build pipeline, a set of scripts that cut each document into separately retrievable pieces, group related pieces, and convert each piece into a numerical representation of its meaning. The pieces produced by the build pipeline are stored in a (3) reference library, a database that can be searched by meaning. When a learner submits a text, a (4) request assembler searches the reference library for the pieces relevant to that text and combines them with fixed instructions into a single request. This request is sent to a (5) large language model (LLM), which generates the feedback and returns it to the learner. Every feedback output, together with the identifiers of the library pieces used to produce it, is stored in a (6) feedback log. The feedback log closes the cycle: the teacher reviews the logged feedback, her critiques produce new or revised expert documents, and the build pipeline adds these to the reference library for the next request. The following paragraphs describe how these components deliver the feedback categories, how they carry the expert knowledge into each request, and how they support the refinement cycle.

**Delivering the feedback categories.** The five linguistic elements are delivered through two requests to the LLM, made at two points in the learner's writing process. The first point is each scene of the story. Before writing a scene, the learner fills in optional planning boxes for the characters, objects, actions, and descriptions the scene will contain; these boxes are scaffolding without AI, and their content is never sent to the LLM. When the learner submits the scene, the request assembler produces a per-scene request, and the LLM returns feedback in four fixed sections: Grammar Corrections, Vocabulary Suggestions, Coherence & Expressiveness, and Encouragement. These sections cover four of the five elements, and the learner may revise the scene once in response. The fifth element, content completeness, can only be judged once all scenes exist, because one required scene of a story may be told across several of the learner's scenes. Content completeness is therefore judged at the second point, the end of the story, through a separate end-of-story request that returns two sections: Content & Completeness, and Overall Encouragement. Each element is matched to its own expert reference: the teacher's corrections for grammar and coherence, the vocabulary error list for vocabulary, the model story for expressiveness, and the required-scene list for content completeness.

**Carrying expert knowledge into each request.** Matching each element to its expert reference requires that the reference reach the LLM at the right time. Every request consists of two messages. The system message contains standing instructions that are identical for every learner text: the feedback rules, the coherence rules, the rule for vocabulary errors, and the output format. The user message contains the material that changes from text to text: the library pieces selected for the learner's text, followed by the text itself. Which message a piece of knowledge goes into is decided by one question: does the knowledge apply to every text, or only to some (Table 3)? Knowledge that applies to every text is written into the system message, and is supported by examples that are included in every user message. Knowledge that applies only to some texts is retrieved from the reference library for each text. Knowledge that belongs to one story, such as its model story or required-scene list, is looked up by the story being retold.

Retrieval for each text works in two ways. The main way is retrieval by meaning. During the build, each library piece was converted into an embedding, a list of numbers representing its meaning, so that pieces with similar meanings have similar embeddings. When a learner submits a scene, the scene is converted into an embedding in the same way, and the reference library returns the pieces whose embeddings are closest to it. Retrieval by meaning finds pieces related to what the learner wrote, which are not always the rules the learner broke. A learner who omits 了 may write nothing that resembles the teacher's 了 corrections. For this reason, the pieces for the problems the teacher corrected most often (character introduction, ambiguous reference, and 了) are included in every request rather than retrieved. The second way is retrieval by wording, used for the vocabulary error list. A vocabulary error is a specific short word form, such as 找 used to mean "find", and the meaning of a whole scene does not reliably bring out one short form within it. Each entry in the vocabulary error list therefore carries a trigger pattern, such as 找 not followed by 到, and every entry whose pattern occurs in the scene is included. The three entries closest in meaning are added as well, so that the same error in other words is also caught.

*Table 3.* Routing of expert knowledge to the LLM

| Route | Content | Included when | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| System message | Feedback rules; coherence rules; rule for vocabulary errors; output format | Every request | Applies to every text |
| Always included in the user message | 8 cards of past AI errors; 3 rule cards (character introduction, ambiguous reference, 了) | Every scene | Examples behind the rules; most frequent problems |
| Retrieved by meaning | 4 rule cards; 3 teacher corrections (same story first in a retelling) | Closest in meaning to the scene | Applies only to some texts |

| Looked up by story | Model story (each scene); required-scene list (end of story) | Retellings only | Belongs to one story |

**Supporting the refinement cycle.** The same components that carry expert knowledge into each request also make the refinement cycle possible, in three ways. First, the system is updatable. A critique is addressed by changing one expert document or one instruction, and when a document changes, the build pipeline processes only that document again; the next request uses the new knowledge. No part of the LLM is retrained, and the teacher's own wording reaches the LLM without being rewritten by the developer. Second, the system is traceable. Because the feedback log stores the identifiers of every library piece used for each output, the exact input behind any feedback item can be reconstructed. When the teacher critiques a feedback item, this record shows whether the LLM lacked the relevant knowledge, which calls for a change to the reference library, or had the knowledge but did not apply it, which calls for a change to the instructions. Third, the system is separable. Retrieval from the reference library can be switched off while the instructions stay the same, so the contribution of each component can be tested on its own. This separability is what makes the controlled comparison in Section 3.6 possible.

All three properties follow from the decision to change what the LLM reads rather than the LLM itself. Changing the LLM itself, by fine-tuning its parameters on training examples, is the basis of recent hybrid approaches to essay scoring (Section 2.3). Those approaches are trained on thousands of scored essays, whereas this study has 103 annotated retellings from one teacher. A fine-tuned model would also store the teacher's knowledge in its parameters, where it can be neither traced nor edited by the teacher. Retrieval keeps that knowledge in readable documents, which is what allows it to be updated, traced, and tested.

**Technical specification.** The components were implemented as follows. The build pipeline is a set of Python and Node.js scripts; it produced 1,269 library pieces of nine types, listed in Appendix C, and grouped corrections into rule cards and verdicts into cards of past errors using keyword rules that match the teacher's own shorthand (coverage: 32% of corrections and 81% of verdicts). Embeddings are produced by text-embedding-3-small (OpenAI; 1,536 dimensions), and the reference library is a MongoDB Atlas database searched with Atlas Vector Search, using cosine similarity, filters by piece type and story, and at least 100 candidates per search. The LLM is gpt-5.6-terra (OpenAI), accessed through the OpenAI API between [dates]. This is a reasoning model, run with reasoning effort set to low; it does not allow the temperature to be fixed, so the same text can receive different feedback on different runs, a variation measured in Section 3.6. Earlier versions of the system used Llama 3.3 70B through the Groq API (Section 3.5). The learner interface is a Next.js web application hosted on Vercel. The feedback log stores, for each scene, the feedback, the identifiers of the library pieces used, the learner's original, revised, and final text, and the start and completion times; the model version returned with each request is also logged [to do: implement before data collection].
